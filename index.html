What is Risk?

Let's start from scratch:
what is a risk?

A risk is the possibility that something goes wrong — and when it does, it hurts: financially, operationally, or for our customers.
Think of it like driving to work. There is always a risk of an accident. You cannot eliminate that risk — but you wear a seatbelt, follow traffic rules, and do not text while driving. You manage the risk. That is exactly what we do at Nubank.
→
Risk is not the same as a problem. It is the possibility of one — it may or may not happen.
→
Risk is not something to eliminate. Every business takes risk. The goal is to take it consciously and within limits.
→
Our job: identify, understand, and manage risks before they become real problems.

Three types of risk

TypeIn plain EnglishExample in our world
Operational Risk Something in our process, system or team fails A service goes down during peak hours because no one owned the recovery playbook
Security Risk Someone unauthorized accesses or damages our data or systems A password gets exposed in a code repo and someone uses it to access production
Regulatory & Compliance Risk We fail to follow a rule that regulators or auditors require — and face consequences BACEN requests evidence of how a credit decision was made. Lending Foundations operates under BACEN and Febraban in Brazil, plus equivalent regulators in Mexico and Colombia. And because Nubank is a publicly traded company, we are also subject to SOX (Sarbanes-Oxley) — which means our financial controls and data trails are audited by external auditors like KPMG every year. A data or process failure here is not just a technical incident — it can become a regulatory event across multiple frameworks simultaneously.

What are Controls?

So what do we do about risk?
We build controls.

A control is anything we put in place to reduce the chance of something going wrong — or to reduce the damage when it does.
Back to driving: a traffic light prevents accidents. A seatbelt corrects the impact when one happens. A speed camera detects when someone breaks the rules. None eliminates risk — but together they make the road much safer.
→
Controls are not bureaucracy. They are what allows us to move fast without breaking things that matter.
→
A control that exists on paper but is not followed is not a control. It is just a document.
What are Controls? · Types

Three types of controls —
you already use all of them.

TypeGoalReal example in Foundations
PreventiveStop the problem before it happensMandatory PR review; staging validation before prod; approval before changing a critical config
DetectiveCatch the problem when happeningDashboard showing payment rates dropping in real time; automated test catching a broken pipeline instantly; weekly report flagging unusual loan state transitions
CorrectiveMinimize damage and recover fastA playbook with step-by-step recovery instructions; a rollback procedure any engineer can follow
Preventive controls are always cheaper than corrective ones. It is far easier to stop a failure than to recover from one.
Security Risk

Security risk, explained simply.

Security risk means: someone who should not have access to something — gets it. Or something that should be reliable — gets broken or stolen.
Imagine leaving your house key under the doormat. Even if nothing bad happens today, the risk is real. Now imagine that key opens the home of every single Nubank customer. That is the scale of what we are protecting.
1
Never put passwords or keys in code. Even temporarily. Even in a private repo. Use the secrets manager.
2
Do not leave customer data in plain sight. Names, document numbers, loan amounts should never appear in logs unprotected.
3
Only ask for the access you actually need. Less access means less risk.
4
When something looks off — say something. Early signal always beats late confirmation.

Security in practice.

Situation
While reviewing a pull request, you notice a colleague accidentally committed a database connection string — including username and password — to the repo. It was merged an hour ago.
Not quite. Deleting the commit does not revoke the credential — it is already exposed. Handling it privately means the team cannot assess whether it was accessed.
Correct. The credential must be rotated immediately regardless of whether it was accessed. Surface it fast and do not try to handle it alone.
"Private repo" is not a safe assumption. Repos can be cloned, leaked, or accidentally made public. Credentials in version history are a known attack vector.
Tech Debt as Risk

Tech debt is not just a dev problem.
It is a risk problem.

Tech debt is what happens when we take shortcuts today to move faster — and those shortcuts pile up into fragility over time.
Think of it like a messy kitchen. One unwashed dish is fine. But if you never clean up, eventually you cannot cook anything — and when you finally need the kitchen, it takes three times longer. In platform teams, that mess shows up as incidents, outages, and audit findings.
→
A service processing thousands of payments daily, built 3 years ago, with no documentation and architecture never updated. One unexpected spike and it falls over — and no one knows where to start.
→
A data pipeline feeding credit decisions, built with a workaround that was "supposed to be temporary." Nobody remembers why it works that way. If it breaks, we cannot explain our decisions to regulators.
→
A critical config change that requires one specific engineer to do it manually every time. That engineer is the single point of failure — and they do not even know it.
BCO is structuring a Tech Debt project for Lending Foundations
We are building a structured approach to make tech debt visible, prioritized, and tracked as real risk. Starting in 2027, this initiative will give the BU a clear framework to address our most critical areas before they become incidents or audit findings.
